## Euler Angles: Three Rotations Applied in Sequence

Any rotation in 3D space has exactly 3 degrees of freedom — a $3\times 3$ matrix has 9 entries but they're always constrained so that 3 independent numbers fully describe it. Euler angles package those 3 numbers in the most intuitive way: split one general rotation (hard to visualize directly) into three simpler rotations — each spinning around a **single axis** — then compose them one after another. Those three angles are usually called roll/pitch/yaw in aviation, or simply the angle around $X$, $Y$, $Z$.

Each single-axis rotation is a familiar matrix from the Matrices & Transforms lesson:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_x(\alpha)&=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\\0&\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{pmatrix}\\[6pt]
R_y(\beta)&=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\beta&0&\sin\beta\\0&1&0\\-\sin\beta&0&\cos\beta\end{pmatrix}\\[6pt]
R_z(\gamma)&=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\gamma&-\sin\gamma&0\\\sin\gamma&\cos\gamma&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

An Euler rotation with order `"XYZ"` (Three.js's `Euler` default) is the product of these three matrices in one specific order. That "specific order" — not the three angles themselves — is where every headache in the rest of this lesson comes from.

## Axis Order Decides the Outcome: XYZ vs ZYX

Matrix multiplication doesn't commute, so applying the exact same two rotations in different orders lands you in different final orientations. Example: take $v=(1,0,0)$, rotate $90°$ about $X$ then $90°$ about $Y$, versus $Y$ first then $X$:

```ts
type Vec3 = [number, number, number];

function rotateX(v: Vec3, a: number): Vec3 {
  const [x, y, z] = v;
  return [x, y * Math.cos(a) - z * Math.sin(a), y * Math.sin(a) + z * Math.cos(a)];
}
function rotateY(v: Vec3, a: number): Vec3 {
  const [x, y, z] = v;
  return [x * Math.cos(a) + z * Math.sin(a), y, -x * Math.sin(a) + z * Math.cos(a)];
}

const v: Vec3 = [1, 0, 0];
const rad = Math.PI / 2;
rotateY(rotateX(v, rad), rad); // X first, then Y → [0, 0, -1]
rotateX(rotateY(v, rad), rad); // Y first, then X → [0, 1, 0]
```

The same pair of $90°/90°$ angles, applied in swapped order, produces $(0,0,-1)$ or $(0,1,0)$ — two perpendicular directions, not a rounding error. That's why `THREE.Euler` always carries an `order`: two objects with identical `rotation.x/y/z` but different `order` face different ways.

## Intrinsic vs Extrinsic: Rotating About Which Axis

**Extrinsic**: all three axes are fixed world axes, none of them move between steps. **Intrinsic**: each later axis is measured on the frame already tilted by the previous step — the $Y$ axis used in step two is the object's $Y$ axis *after* the $X$ rotation, not the original world $Y$. A real mechanical gimbal stack (a ship's compass) is the physical embodiment of intrinsic composition: the outer ring rotating drags the middle and inner rings along.

`THREE.Euler`'s default order `"XYZ"` uses the intrinsic convention: rotate local $X$ first, local $Y$ second (already tilted by the previous step), local $Z$ last (tilted by both previous steps). In matrix terms this equals applying $R_x$ to the vector first — $R = R_z(\gamma)R_y(\beta)R_x(\alpha)$, matching the example above. The full table for all 12 possible orders lives in Eberly's document in the References — no need to memorize it, just remember: changing `order` changes the actual math, not just a label.

## Gimbal Lock: When the Middle Rotation Hits ±90°, a Degree of Freedom Vanishes

Rewrite the composition above with familiar names — $\alpha$ = roll (X), $\beta$ = pitch (Y, the **middle** axis), $\gamma$ = yaw (Z):

$$
R(\alpha,\beta,\gamma) = R_z(\gamma)\,R_y(\beta)\,R_x(\alpha)
$$

At $\beta=90°$: $\cos\beta=0$, $\sin\beta=1$. Multiplying the three matrices out, every term with $\cos\beta$ vanishes, collapsing to:

$$
R(\alpha,90°,\gamma) =
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & \sin(\alpha-\gamma) & \cos(\alpha-\gamma)\\
0 & \cos(\alpha-\gamma) & -\sin(\alpha-\gamma)\\
-1 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
$$

$\alpha$ and $\gamma$ no longer appear individually, only their difference $\alpha-\gamma$ decides the matrix. Roll and yaw — two axes meant to be independent — collapse into **one** shared variable once pitch hits $90°$; the original three degrees of freedom are down to two. Physically: once the middle ring (pitch) has turned a full $90°$, the inner ring's rotation axis (roll, already tilted by pitch) becomes parallel to the outer ring's axis (yaw, fixed to the world) — turning either ring produces the exact same motion.

### A Numeric Check: Two Different Angle Pairs, One Matrix

The pairs $(\alpha,\gamma)=(60°,25°)$ and $(\alpha,\gamma)=(10°,-25°)$ both have $\alpha-\gamma=35°$. With $\sin 35°\approx 0.574$, $\cos 35°\approx 0.819$, both produce the exact same matrix:

$$
R(\alpha, 90°, \gamma) \approx
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0.574 & 0.819 \\ 0 & 0.819 & -0.574 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Two entirely different Euler triples describe **the same physical orientation** — the direct consequence of $\beta=90°$ erasing a dimension of information, not a numerical coincidence.

## Why Interpolating Euler Poses Can Swing Unexpectedly

Lerping three numbers directly ($\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ each by $t$) doesn't respect the coupled geometry of rotation space shown above. Near $\beta\approx 90°$, the matrix depends only on $\alpha-\gamma$ — keeping the displayed orientation smooth requires $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ to drift together; independent per-channel lerp has no idea about that, so the object can crawl at an odd pace and then suddenly speed up, or swing through a strange intermediate orientation even though start and end look "close." A second, more mundane source of swings: lerping an angle straight across the $0°/360°$ boundary — naively lerping $350°\to10°$ sweeps roughly $340°$ the long way instead of the short $20°$. Both stem from applying linear interpolation — built for flat scalars — onto a space that isn't flat; the next lesson's quaternions fix exactly this with slerp.

## When Euler Is Enough, When You Need Something Else

Euler works fine when only 2 of 3 axes get used and the remaining axis is **hard-clamped** away from $90°$: a first-person camera only needs pitch (look up/down) and yaw (turn left/right), no roll, pitch clamped to a safe range like $[-89°,89°]$ — never reaching the lock point. Euler breaks down once an object can freely rotate about all three axes with no limits: acrobatic flight simulation (drones, spacecraft tumbling freely), or animation blending between arbitrary poses of a skeletal rig — both eventually carry the orientation through the $\beta=90°$ zone, and interpolating between Euler keyframes swings exactly as described above.

## Where This Leads: Quaternions and Animation

The next lesson replaces Euler with quaternions — no notion of a "second axis, third axis," so gimbal lock can't happen, and genuinely smooth interpolation (slerp) instead of per-angle lerp. From Track 3 on, every `Object3D` stores rotation in both `object.rotation` (Euler, convenient for hand-editing) and `object.quaternion` (the real source used to build the matrix, kept in sync) — `AnimationMixer` always interpolates keyframes using quaternions, never Euler, for exactly the reason covered above.

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Common mistakes">

The three most common Euler-angle mistakes:

1. **Assuming `rotation.x/y/z` are three independent numbers.** Changing `object.rotation.order` while keeping `x, y, z` unchanged still changes the final orientation — order decides which sequence $R_x,R_y,R_z$ multiply in, not just a descriptive label.
2. **Lerping an angle straight across the $0°/360°$ boundary.** Naively lerping $350°\to10°$ tends to sweep through roughly $340°$ (the long way) instead of $20°$ (the short way) — wrap the angle difference into $(-180°,180°]$ before lerping.
3. **Treating gimbal lock as a software bug to "fix" with more precision.** It's a structural property of ANY rotation representation built from three sequential single-axis rotations — `float64` doesn't escape it either, because the loss is algebraic (a degree of freedom vanishing at $\beta=90°$), not a rounding error. The only real fix is switching representations, exactly what the next lesson does with quaternions.

</Callout>
