## The Chain From Model to Clip Space: What Each Matrix Does

Every vertex that ever reaches your screen passes through exactly one chain of matrix multiplications:

$$
\mathbf{p}_{clip} = P \, V \, M \, \mathbf{p}_{model}
$$

Read it right to left — the order the GPU actually applies it, since vectors are columns multiplied from the right: $M$ places a single object into the scene's shared coordinates; $V$ rewrites that whole scene from the camera's point of view; $P$ warps the space around the camera into the volume hardware knows how to clip, setting up the perspective divide. Most engines fold $VM$ into a single `modelViewMatrix` first, avoiding re-multiplying $M$ per vertex — the line you already used in the previous track's demo shader is this exact chain, written out: `gl_Position = projectionMatrix * modelViewMatrix * vec4(position, 1.0);`, where `modelViewMatrix` is $VM$ and `projectionMatrix` is $P$.

The multiplication order isn't an arbitrary convention: swapping it to $MVP$, or reordering any pair, produces a completely different transform — not "slightly off," but an object that vanishes from the screen or warps beyond recognition.

## Six Spaces, Six Concrete Meanings

<Figure
  src="/figures/00-math/mvp-transform-chain.svg"
  alt="Vertical chain of coordinate spaces from local space down to screen pixels, each arrow labelled with the matrix or hardware step that performs that hop"
  caption="Only the first three hops are matrices you supply; the perspective divide and the viewport transform run automatically after the vertex shader."
/>

- **Model space**: coordinates local to the mesh itself, origin usually at its center or base — a cube modeled at $[-0.5, 0.5]^3$ has no idea yet where it will stand in the scene.
- **World space**: after multiplying by $M$, every object, light and camera shares one coordinate frame — say $M$ translates that cube to $(3, 0, -5)$; it now genuinely sits there in the shared world.
- **View space (camera space)**: after multiplying by $V$, the same coordinates are rewritten with the camera sitting at the origin, looking down $-z$ — distance along $z$ here is literally "how far in front of the camera," the exact value the perspective formula below needs.
- **Clip space**: after multiplying by $P$, a 4D homogeneous coordinate $(x, y, z, w)$ — the GPU clips triangles against $-w \le x, y, z \le w$ right here, before dividing; that clipping step is where the name "clip space" comes from.
- **NDC**: after dividing by $w$ — the one nonlinear step in the entire chain — the clip volume becomes a genuine $[-1,1]^3$ cube, simple to compare and clip further.
- **Screen/viewport space**: NDC remapped to actual render-target pixels via `gl.viewport()` — a fully separate 2D affine step, not part of the projection matrix at all.

## The View Matrix Is the Camera's Transform, Inverted

A camera in the scene has a transform just like any other object — its position and orientation form a "camera-to-world" matrix, call it $C$. The single most confusable point in this lesson: the view matrix isn't $C$, it's $C^{-1}$. The reason is purely geometric: $C$ answers "which world point does this camera-space point correspond to," while what you actually need is the opposite — "what does the world look like from the camera." Inverting the matrix is exactly that reversal.

This is the root of the classic intuition "the camera moves left, the world drifts right": if $C$ translates the camera $+3$ units along $x$, then $C^{-1}$ translates every world point $-3$ along $x$ — the camera perceives itself as sitting still at the origin, while the entire world slides the opposite direction it just moved. Rotation works the same way: the inverse of an orthonormal rotation $R$ is $R^\top$ (the transpose) — spinning the world the exact opposite way the camera just turned.

## Building the lookAt Matrix From Eye, Target and Up

The practical way to get $C$ isn't typing 16 numbers by hand — it's building it from three orthogonal vectors, exactly what every engine's `lookAt(eye, target, up)` does:

$$
\hat f = \widehat{eye - target}, \qquad \hat r = \widehat{up \times \hat f}, \qquad \hat u = \hat f \times \hat r
$$

```ts
function lookAtBasis(eye: Vec3, target: Vec3, worldUp: Vec3) {
  const forward = normalize(sub(eye, target)); // points AWAY from the view direction (right-hand convention)
  const right = normalize(cross(worldUp, forward));
  const up = cross(forward, right); // exactly perpendicular, even if worldUp wasn't
  return { right, up, forward };
}
```

$\hat f$ ("forward," pointing opposite the view direction under the right-handed convention) is the negated look direction; $\hat r$ is built from the cross of a temporary up and $\hat f$ — the same cross-product technique from the previous lesson; $\hat u$ is simply the cross of the other two, guaranteeing all three are exactly perpendicular even when `worldUp` isn't perfectly perpendicular to the view direction. These three vectors are $C$'s first three columns; the fourth column is `eye`.

## The Perspective Matrix at the Formula Level: fov, Focal Length, Near/Far

The actual "perspective" lives in four numbers of $P$:

$$
P = \begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{f}{\text{aspect}} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & f & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & \dfrac{\text{far}+\text{near}}{\text{near}-\text{far}} & \dfrac{2\,\text{far}\,\text{near}}{\text{near}-\text{far}} \\
0 & 0 & -1 & 0
\end{bmatrix}, \qquad f = \frac{1}{\tan(\text{fov}/2)}
$$

$f$ acts as "focal length": the smaller the fov (a zoomed-in lens), the smaller $\tan(\text{fov}/2)$, the larger $f$, the "tighter" and bigger objects appear. A $60°$ fov gives $f = 1/\tan(30°) \approx 1.732$; a $90°$ fov gives $f = 1/\tan(45°) = 1$ — a much wider angle pulls $f$ down toward 1. The bottom row $(0, 0, -1, 0)$ is the most commonly overlooked detail: it copies $-z_{view}$ straight into $w_{clip}$, and $w$ is exactly the denominator of the upcoming perspective divide — the farther an object is (the larger $-z_{view}$), the larger $w$, and the more the post-divide coordinate shrinks. That's the entire mechanism behind distant objects looking smaller. The third row compresses the $[\text{near}, \text{far}]$ range (nonlinearly, as $1/z$) into $[-1, 1]$ of $z_{ndc}$ — NDC depth isn't linear with real-world depth, a fact that causes bugs whenever depth gets compared by eye instead of by number.

## From NDC to Pixels: the Viewport Transform

The last step is just a scale-and-shift, identical to what you did in the UV space lesson, only flipped:

$$
x_{screen} = (x_{ndc} \cdot 0.5 + 0.5)\, W, \qquad y_{screen} = \big(1 - (y_{ndc} \cdot 0.5 + 0.5)\big)\, H
$$

```js
function ndcToScreen(xNdc, yNdc, W, H) {
  return {
    x: (xNdc * 0.5 + 0.5) * W,
    y: (1 - (yNdc * 0.5 + 0.5)) * H, // flip y: NDC points up, pixels point down
  };
}

ndcToScreen(0.5, -0.5, 800, 600); // { x: 600, y: 450 }
```

$W, H$ are the viewport's pixel dimensions; the $y$ axis still needs flipping because NDC has $y$ pointing up while screen pixels have $y$ pointing down — the exact problem you already solved in Cartesian & UV Space, just with different variable names.

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Common mistakes">

The three most common mistakes with the model-view-projection chain:

1. **Multiplying the matrices in the wrong order.** three.js is column-major and multiplies column vectors from the right, so the correct expression is always $P V M \mathbf{p}$. Writing $MVP$ instead, or swapping any two matrices, produces a completely different transform — usually an object that vanishes from the screen or warps beyond recognition, not something merely "slightly off."
2. **Forgetting to divide by $w$ after multiplying by $P$.** Clip space is still an unnormalized 4D coordinate; comparing $x, y, z$ directly against $[-1, 1]$ before dividing by $w$ is always wrong, because $w$ equals $-z_{view}$, not the constant 1.
3. **Using the camera's own transform matrix ($C$) as the view matrix instead of $C^{-1}$.** The camera moves right, and the code sees the whole world drift right too, in the same direction, instead of the opposite one — a bug that's hard to spot by eye because the scene still "moves," just in the wrong direction.

</Callout>
