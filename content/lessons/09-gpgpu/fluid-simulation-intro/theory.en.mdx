## Three Approaches to Real-Time Fluid Simulation

The cloth lesson before this one was a finite grid of points tied to their neighbors by distance constraints — a tidy model that runs well on a state texture. Fluid is harder because its constraint is **global**, not local: volume has to stay conserved everywhere at once, not just between one neighbor pair. Two completely different families solve that — **particle-based** (SPH, PBF) and **grid-based** (Jos Stam's Stable Fluids) — and this lesson only maps those three directions, it doesn't fully implement any of them. The demo below builds the one corner that's actually feasible in a browser tab: 2D Stable Fluids on a grid.

## SPH — Particles Carrying Mass, Density from a Smoothing Kernel

Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics treats fluid as a scattered cloud of particles, each carrying mass $m$, position, velocity. There's no grid at all — density at particle $i$ is estimated by summing contributions from nearby particles, weighted down by distance through a **smoothing kernel function** $W$ (typically a cubic spline or poly6, with support radius $h$):

$$
\rho_i = \sum_j m_j\, W(\lVert \mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j\rVert, h)
$$

Pressure at $i$ follows from how far its density deviates from rest density ($p_i \propto \rho_i - \rho_0$), and the pressure gradient (also derived from $W$'s derivative) pushes compressed particles apart. The expensive part isn't the math — it's **finding neighbors**: the sum above requires scanning every particle pair within radius $h$, and brute force is $O(N^2)$. At $N=5000$ particles that's 25 million distance checks per frame; at 100,000 it's 10 billion — without a spatial acceleration structure (spatial hashing, uniform grid binning), even a "modest" particle count is enough to kill your framerate. SPH is the natural fit for fluid with **visible surfaces and splashes** — pouring water, waves, droplets — because every particle is naturally a surface sample.

## PBF — Position Constraints Instead of Forces

Position Based Fluids (Macklin & Müller, 2013) keeps SPH's density formula but changes how it's used: instead of accumulating pressure forces and integrating velocity, PBF writes density as a **position constraint** every particle must satisfy:

$$
C_i(\mathbf{p}) = \frac{\rho_i}{\rho_0} - 1 = 0
$$

then solves that constraint directly on positions — the exact Position-Based Dynamics technique the cloth lesson used for distance constraints, just with a different constraint shape. "Solve, don't integrate forces" is why PBF stays stable at larger timesteps where classic force-based SPH doesn't: SPH needs a high stiffness $k$ to keep fluid "dense" enough, and a high $k$ is exactly what triggers explosive numerical oscillation. This is the technique behind most fluid particle systems in modern game engines (NVIDIA Flex, PhysX fluid).

## Stam's Stable Fluids — a Grid, Not Particles

The third direction drops the particle concept entirely. Space is divided into a fixed grid, each cell storing velocity (and, depending on the application, smoke density, temperature, or dye color) — exactly the state-texture pattern used throughout this track. Jos Stam (1999) reduced incompressible Navier–Stokes to a four-step update loop per frame: add external force, **advect** (move each quantity along the velocity field itself), diffuse, **project** (force the velocity field divergence-free).

The advect step is what earns the name "stable": Stam uses **semi-Lagrangian advection** — for each cell, trace backward along velocity to find where that cell's content "came from" last frame, then sample there with interpolation — instead of forward (explicit Euler) integration, which blows up numerically once $\Delta t$ exceeds the CFL condition. Backward tracing stays stable for **any** $\Delta t$, at the cost of numerical diffusion (fine detail fades over time even with dissipation turned all the way off) — stable is not the same thing as accurate.

The project step is exactly the divergence from the curl-noise lesson (Track 7): Helmholtz decomposition says any field $\mathbf{w}$ splits into a divergence-free part plus a pressure gradient, $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + \nabla p$. Solve for $p$ via the Poisson equation $\nabla^2 p = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{w}$, then subtract:

$$
\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{w} - \nabla p
$$

There's no closed-form solution on a discrete grid, so $p$ is solved with **Jacobi** iteration — each pass, every cell updates from its four neighbors and its own fixed divergence value:

```glsl
// one Jacobi relaxation step inside the pressure solve
float p = (pLeft + pRight + pBottom + pTop - divergence) * 0.25;
```

More iterations pull the field closer to genuinely divergence-free — exactly the slider in the demo below.

## Why the Famous Web "Fluid" Demos Are All 2D Stable Fluids

The WebGL demo in Pavel Dobryakov's style (the `PavelDoGreat/WebGL-Fluid-Simulation` repo) that nearly everyone learning GLSL has seen — that's not SPH. It's Stable Fluids on a 2D grid: a "dye" texture advected by a velocity texture, mouse drags splatting force directly into both. It dominates web demos not for aesthetic reasons — for cost: a fixed grid needs no neighbor search (SPH's $O(N^2)$ problem simply doesn't exist here, sampling a neighboring texel is always $O(1)$), and every step (advect, splat, divergence, Jacobi, project) is a fullscreen-quad fragment shader reading/writing an FBO — exactly the GPGPU ping-pong foundation this track opened with. No new tool required, just more passes.

## Cost, Complexity, and Picking the Right Tool

| | SPH | PBF | Stable Fluids |
|---|---|---|---|
| Representation | Discrete particles | Discrete particles | Fixed grid (texture) |
| Main cost driver | Neighbor search $O(N^2)$ (no binning) | Neighbor search + constraint solve iterations | Grid cells × steps × Jacobi iterations |
| Stability | Sensitive to $\Delta t$, stiffness $k$ | More stable than force-based SPH | Unconditionally stable (advect), but numerically diffusive |
| Reasonable web scale | A few thousand particles without binning | Similar to SPH, needs binning to reach tens of thousands | 128²–256² grid, smooth at 60fps |
| Best for | Fluid with surfaces, splashes, droplets | Same as SPH, needs more stability | Smoke, ink, 2D screen-space effects |

## The Honest Scope on the Web

Bluntly: a production-quality 3D liquid simulation — water pouring in a game, splash VFX — is out of scope for a browser tab. It needs real SPH/PBF with spatial hashing, tens of thousands of particles holding 60fps, a GPU budget that's reasonable on console/native but not reasonable to compete for against the rest of a webpage. A 2D grid is the web's sweet spot: visually convincing enough (smoke, ink, mouse-reactive effects), cheap enough to leave room for everything else on the page. The demo below builds exactly that sweet spot — not because SPH/PBF are "too hard," but because it's the right engineering choice for this environment. <Callout variant="mistake" title="Common mistakes">

The three most common mistakes when first reading or attempting a fluid simulation:

1. **Blaming "the GPU is slow" when the algorithm is actually $O(N^2)$.** A brute-force SPH with 5000 particles tanking framerate isn't a weak GPU — it's the missing spatial structure (uniform grid/spatial hash) that would limit neighbor search to a real local neighborhood instead of the entire particle set.
2. **Cutting Jacobi iterations to "save time" without realizing divergence hasn't actually reached zero.** The result looks blocky, weakly swirling, or has no recirculation at all — not a formula bug, just a pressure solution that hasn't converged enough; exactly what the demo's iteration slider demonstrates.
3. **Confusing semi-Lagrangian advection's "unconditional stability" with "accuracy."** Setting dissipation to zero doesn't remove any dye color directly, yet the field still visibly fades over time — that's the inherent numerical diffusion of interpolating at every advect step, not a dissipation bug.

</Callout>
