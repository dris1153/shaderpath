## PerspectiveCamera vs OrthographicCamera: Two Projections, Two Use Cases

Back in Track 0, projection was the third stage of the Model → View → Projection pipeline, turning camera-space coordinates into clip space. Three.js ships two camera classes for two different projection matrices. `PerspectiveCamera(fov, aspect, near, far)` renders with perspective projection: farther objects shrink, and parallel lines converge toward a vanishing point — exactly how human eyes perceive the world, which makes it the default choice for 3D games, architectural walkthroughs, and any scene that needs a real sense of depth. `OrthographicCamera(left, right, top, bottom, near, far)` renders with parallel projection instead: an object's on-screen size stays constant regardless of its distance from the camera, and parallel lines in the world stay parallel on screen.

Orthographic wins in three concrete cases: 3D UI overlays (a 3D icon must keep its exact size even as the main camera pulls back), isometric/strategy games (strategy titles use an orthographic or near-orthographic view so players can compare unit sizes accurately no matter where they sit on the map), and CAD/2.5D blueprints (no distortion when measuring on screen). fov/aspect/near/far on Perspective should look familiar from Track 0; left/right/top/bottom on Orthographic play the same role — they define a box frustum directly instead of a truncated pyramid, and it's that box's dimensions, not the camera's distance, that decide how big an object looks on screen.

## OrbitControls: Target, Damping and the update() Loop

`OrbitControls` (from `three/addons/controls/OrbitControls.js`) is the standard way to let a user orbit around a point. It doesn't replace the camera — it listens for pointer/wheel events on the `domElement` you pass in, then writes directly to `camera.position` and `camera.quaternion` every time you call `controls.update()`. Internally it stores the camera's position as spherical coordinates (radius, azimuthal angle, polar angle) around `controls.target`, a `Vector3` defaulting to `(0,0,0)` that the camera always looks at.

`enableDamping = true` adds inertia: instead of the camera snapping to a stop the instant you release the pointer, it keeps easing along, slowed each frame by `dampingFactor` (default `0.05`). That's why `controls.update()` **must** live inside the render loop rather than only firing on pointer events: during every damped-easing frame, no pointer event happens at all — call `update()` only from an event handler and damping freezes the instant your finger leaves the mouse. Even with damping off, the official docs still recommend calling `update()` every frame as a default habit, because `autoRotate` (covered below) relies on exactly the same mechanism.

## Distance and Angle Clamps: Distance Clamp, Polar Angle Clamp, Pan

Four clamp pairs come up constantly. `minDistance`/`maxDistance` (default `0`/`Infinity`) bound the spherical radius — for a perspective camera that's the dolly-zoom distance; for orthographic, zooming changes `camera.zoom` instead of the radius, so `minZoom`/`maxZoom` are the ones that actually apply. `minPolarAngle`/`maxPolarAngle` (default `0` and `Math.PI`, in radians) clamp the vertical orbit angle — `0` looks straight down from the top, `Math.PI` looks straight up from below; setting `maxPolarAngle` to roughly $\pi/2$ is the classic way to stop a camera from ever dipping below the ground plane. Avoid clamping to exactly `0` or `Math.PI`: at those poles the camera's "up" direction stops being uniquely defined, and controls tend to jitter right at the boundary — leaving a small buffer (say `0.05`) is common practice. `minAzimuthAngle`/`maxAzimuthAngle` do the same job horizontally, unbounded by default.

Panning (`enablePan`, on by default) shifts both `camera.position` and `controls.target` together — unlike dolly or rotate, which only move the camera around a fixed target. `screenSpacePanning` (default `true`) decides whether pan slides along a plane perpendicular to the camera's view direction (feels natural at an angled view) or along the horizontal plane containing `camera.up` (feels natural for a top-down map view) — `MapControls`, covered next, flips this flag to `false`.

## Other Controls: Map, Trackball, Fly, PointerLock

`MapControls` subclasses `OrbitControls` directly and just changes the defaults: left mouse pans (not rotates), right mouse rotates, `screenSpacePanning = false` — a good fit for map/CAD-style apps where the main interaction is sliding the view sideways rather than orbiting. `TrackballControls` drops the notion of a fixed "up" axis entirely: the camera can flip all the way through a pole without the `minPolarAngle`/`maxPolarAngle` clamp OrbitControls enforces — useful for freely spinning an object in space (a molecule, a satellite) where there's no absolute "up" or "down." `FlyControls` simulates a cockpit: WASD moves along the camera's own local axes while the mouse freely steers where it looks — good for flight or spaceship simulations. `PointerLockControls` locks the pointer to the canvas via the Pointer Lock API and only handles the look direction; WASD movement is code you write yourself — the standard foundation for FPS-style controls. Picking one comes down to two questions: is there a fixed "ground" and "up" (Orbit/Map) or not (Trackball); does the user orbit around a point (Orbit) or move freely through space (Fly/PointerLock)?

## Who Owns the Camera: Code-Driven Animation vs Letting Controls Manage It

Animating a camera in code is usually two lines: `camera.position.lerp(targetPos, alpha)` to ease toward a new position, and `camera.lookAt(lookTarget)` to keep it aimed correctly. The trouble starts when `OrbitControls` is also enabled: every `controls.update()` call overwrites `camera.position`/`camera.quaternion` from its own internal spherical state — if your code sets `camera.position` and then `controls.update()` runs right after, controls will yank the camera back to its old spherical position, silently undoing your animation (or fighting it every single frame if both run in the same loop). Only one side should write to the camera transform each frame — two fixes: either flip `controls.enabled = false` while code is driving the camera and re-enable it afterward, or animate `controls.target` instead of `camera.position`/`lookAt` directly, so controls stays the sole owner and recomputes its spherical state for you.

## Resize: Updating Aspect and updateProjectionMatrix

The projection matrix doesn't recompute itself when a container resizes — it's cached in `camera.projectionMatrix`, only refreshed when you explicitly call `updateProjectionMatrix()`. For a perspective camera, the standard sequence is `camera.aspect = width / height; camera.updateProjectionMatrix();`. Orthographic cameras have no `aspect` property at all — you have to recompute `left`/`right` (or `top`/`bottom`) for the new aspect ratio yourself before calling `updateProjectionMatrix()`, or the image stretches by exactly the mismatch between the old and new canvas ratio. Forgetting `updateProjectionMatrix()` is a silent bug: there's no warning, the camera just keeps rendering with the stale aspect ratio until something else happens to call it.

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Common mistakes">

The three most common camera/controls mistakes:

1. **Enabling `enableDamping` but only calling `controls.update()` on pointer events.** The camera eases for a couple of frames right after you release the pointer, then snaps to a dead stop — damping needs `update()` running every frame, including the ones with zero input, not just the ones with an event.
2. **Animating `camera.position` in code while `OrbitControls` stays enabled.** Both sides fight over the camera transform every frame — the camera jitters or snaps back to its old position because `controls.update()` overwrites your animation right after it runs.
3. **Resizing the canvas and forgetting `updateProjectionMatrix()`.** `camera.aspect` (or `left`/`right`/`top`/`bottom` on Orthographic) changes value, but the projection matrix stays stale because it's only recomputed when this call fires explicitly — the image stretches by exactly the mismatch between the old and new aspect ratio.

</Callout>
