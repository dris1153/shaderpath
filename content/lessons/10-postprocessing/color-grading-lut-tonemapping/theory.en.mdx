## Why HDR Images Can't Hit the Screen Directly

Every correct lighting calculation — summing multiple lights, specular highlights, emissive materials — happens in **linear** space, and linear light has no upper bound at 1.0. A metallic surface reflecting a point light straight-on can produce values in the dozens; an emissive material can shine 5-6x brighter than reference white. Track 11 (PBR) digs into where these numbers come from; this lesson only needs the fact that raw render output lives in the **HDR** range — unbounded above.

Screens, though, are bounded: each channel displays exactly $[0,1]$. The naive approach, `clamp(color, 0.0, 1.0)`, chops everything above 1.0 down to exactly 1.0 — a region at 1.05 and a region at 40.0 look identical, flat white, all detail gone. That's why a deliberate transform is needed: **tone mapping**, which compresses the whole HDR range into $[0,1]$ while preserving relative ordering, instead of clipping.

## Tone Mapping Operators: Deliberate Compression

### Reinhard: The Simplest Asymptotic Curve

$$
c' = \frac{E \cdot c}{1 + E \cdot c}
$$

where $c$ is the per-channel linear value and $E$ is `toneMappingExposure`. $x/(1+x)$ approaches 1 but never reaches it — no hard clip point. But three.js applies this formula **per channel independently**, not through shared luminance: a very bright saturated color (red 6.0, blue 0.2) gets squeezed toward 1.0 on both channels once $c \gg 1$, and the relative gap between channels shrinks — the image visibly pales in very bright regions. That's the mechanism behind "desaturates highlights": a mathematical side effect of per-channel asymptotic compression, not a separate desaturation step.

### ACES Filmic: A Fitted Curve, the Industry Default

`ACESFilmicToneMapping` in three.js doesn't run the full reference ACES pipeline (which involves multiple Academy-standard LUTs) — it uses a fitted approximation instead: multiply through the `ACESInputMat` matrix into AP1 space, apply a rational curve approximating the reference RRT+ODT, then multiply back through `ACESOutputMat` into sRGB. The result has a pronounced shoulder and more contrast than Reinhard — highlights roll off smoothly toward white instead of a monotone asymptote. That's why ACES is the default in most modern game engines.

### AgX: Compression in Log-Encoded Space, Less "Blown Out"

Three 0.185 ships `AgXToneMapping`: color passes through an inset matrix, gets compressed in a log-encoded space, then passes back through an outset matrix. Originating from Blender's AgX view transform, its goal is avoiding the "blown-out HDR game" look ACES sometimes produces — AgX restrains saturation in extreme highlights instead of letting it explode. (Three also ships `NeutralToneMapping` — the Khronos PBR Neutral standard — for cases needing strict neutrality, outside this demo's scope.)

### Shoulder and Toe

Borrowed from film photography: the **toe** is the curve segment in shadows, compressing gently to preserve shadow detail; the **shoulder** is the segment in highlights, compressing progressively harder to avoid an abrupt cutoff. A good curve stays close to linear in the midtones and only bends at both ends — exactly the shape of ACES/AgX, unlike Reinhard's monotone curve.

## Architecture: Where Tone Mapping Actually Lives in EffectComposer

The most commonly misunderstood detail: `RenderPass` renders the scene into an internal `WebGLRenderTarget`, not the screen. Reading `WebGLRenderer.js` directly: whenever the current render target isn't `null`, the renderer forces `toneMapping = NoToneMapping` and the color space to the linear working space — regardless of whatever `renderer.toneMapping` is set to. Every intermediate render target inside a composer always holds raw, un-tone-mapped linear HDR data.

Tone mapping actually happens only in `OutputPass` — it reads `renderer.toneMapping`/`toneMappingExposure`, applies the matching formula, then encodes to sRGB, bundling both steps into one pass. The comment in `OutputPass.js` states it plainly: this pass should sit at the end of the chain, and any pass requiring sRGB input must come after it. A composer keeps HDR linear data alive as long as possible, "casting" it down to displayable LDR exactly once, at the very end.

## 3D LUTs: A Color Lookup Table for Grading

A 3D LUT is an $N^3$ grid of cells, each storing an output RGB for a corresponding input RGB — $N=16$ means $4096$ anchor points spread evenly across color space. A color that doesn't land exactly on an anchor gets **trilinear** interpolation from the GPU — a weighted average of the 8 nearest neighboring cells across 3 axes — automatically, as long as the 3D texture uses `LinearFilter`. Real LUTs are usually baked from professional grading tools into `.cube` files; this lesson generates LUTs from code instead, since the repo ships no binary assets — `lut-generator.ts` computes the teal-orange and bleach-bypass formulas and packs them into a `THREE.Data3DTexture`.

There are two ways to store a LUT on the GPU. A **real 3D texture** (`sampler3D`, WebGL2) is the modern approach — the GPU trilinear-interpolates automatically, sampling is a single `texture(lut, uvw)` call. Before 3D textures were reliably available, the older trick used a **2D strip**: a $32^3$ LUT flattened into a $1024 \times 32$ image (32 tiled $32\times32$ slices), faking the third axis by computing the two neighboring slices from the blue channel, sampling both with a regular `texture2D`, then lerping between them by hand — noticeably more math, but it ran on hardware without 3D textures. This lesson's demo uses a real `Data3DTexture`.

Either way, sampling always needs a **half-texel inset** — the same reason Track 0 added $0.5$ when mapping a pixel to UV, just extended across three axes: $uvw = rgb \cdot (1 - \frac1N) + \frac{1}{2N}$, guaranteeing $rgb=0$ and $rgb=1$ land exactly on the corner cell's center instead of the texture's outer edge.

## Order Doctrine: Grade After Tone Mapping

A grading LUT — whether baked from real camera footage or hand-authored like teal-orange here — is designed for **display-referred** images: values already tone mapped, sitting neatly in $[0,1]$, where "0.2" genuinely means "shadow region." Grade directly onto linear HDR data instead (which could be 40.0), and a $16^3$ LUT covering only $[0,1]$ has nothing to do but clamp it into the nearest edge cell — every highlight, no matter how different, lands in the exact same cell, and the shoulder tone mapping was supposed to produce never happens before grading sees it.

That's why the demo's `GradingPipeline` places `OutputPass` BEFORE the custom LUT pass: `RenderPass` (linear HDR) → `OutputPass` (tone map + sRGB encode) → LUT pass (reading an already-display-referred image). Filament's tone mapping documentation confirms this exact ordering for every modern pipeline.

## The Neutral LUT: A Mandatory Sanity Test

The "neutral" LUT in the demo is an identity mapping — sounds useless, but it's the single most important debugging tool for a LUT pipeline: pick neutral and the image still shifts color, and the bug is guaranteed to be in the sampling math (wrong axis, missing inset), not the LUT's content.

The next lesson dissects the exact `Pass` class `OutputPass`/`ShaderPass` inherit from, writing one from scratch. Locking in the RenderPass → OutputPass → grading order here is the foundation for understanding why pass order matters so much once you're inserting your own processing step into the middle of a chain.

<Callout variant="mistake" title="Common mistakes">

The three most common tone mapping and LUT mistakes:

1. **Grading before tone mapping (LUT pass before OutputPass).** A $16^3$ LUT covers only $[0,1]$; feed it linear HDR values (possibly 40.0) and every highlight clamps into the same edge cell regardless of how different they are — the teal-orange shift never reaches a real highlight, because "highlight" in the display-referred sense doesn't exist yet at that stage.
2. **Forgetting the half-texel inset when sampling a LUT.** Sampling raw `rgb` without $rgb \cdot (1-\frac1N) + \frac1{2N}$ pushes the extremes (pure black/white) outside the cell grid — with `ClampToEdgeWrapping` that lands on a cell that isn't the true corner, a small but systematic error across the whole image.
3. **Treating the neutral LUT as decoration, not a test.** A swapped-axis bug (R and B sampled in the wrong place) or a wrong inset formula can still look "close enough" with a colored LUT — only the neutral LUT catches it immediately, since it's the one case required to reproduce the original pixel exactly.

</Callout>
